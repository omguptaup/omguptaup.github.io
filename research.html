Research

My primary research focus is on Fast Radio Bursts, which were only discovered in 2007 and are a very exciting transient astrophysical phenomena. I am broadly interested in high-energy astrophysics and neutron stars, plasma physics, and cosmology. I work at the intersection of astrophysics and laser-plasma interactions, basically investigating how they propagate through extreme plasma conditions near the source as well as through plasma in the interstellar medium of galaxies. Using analytical calculations, self-developed numerical codes, and particle-in-cell (PIC) simulations, I figure out how the FRB-plasma interaction imprints signatures on the FRB that can be seen in observations. 

Anton Pannekoek Institute, University of Amsterdam

Alex Cooper & Prof. Ralph Wijers
Jun. - Aug. 2021
The largest FRB survey at the time had discovered around 500 FRBs (out of 600 total ever found). This created a uniform sample with the same instrument biases, which made it the most sought after choice for researchers to perform population-level studies. The most popular questions to probe with this sample, were to find the distribution of FRBs as a function of their intrinsic brightness (the luminosity function), and to find how the overall number of FRBs above a brightness threshold has evolved over the past few billion years. The latter question arises because FRBs are thought to arise from a particular type of dead stars, called magnetars, which are created either by the death of massive, young stars on timescales of a few million years, or by the merger of binary inspiralling neutron stars on timescales of few hundred million years, after fresh star formation has happened. We have a good handle on how many new stars form in the Universe as a function of time. Understanding whether the number of FRBs waxes and wanes with the overall star formation in the Universe or has a delay of hundreds of millions of years would provide us with constraints on the evolutionary pathways for FRBs. The former question, regarding intrinsic brightness, provides constraints on the energy injection mechanisms at the source, which can constrain the physics which leads to these bursts. However, the 500 FRB sample in question has a limitation that one of the two observables is a lower limit. Not having accurate data to constrain the intrinsic brightnesses is a major disadvantage, especially when the lower limits are typically not even close to the actual values. The same survey had a smaller sample of 120 bursts with accurate brightness data, which no one had used to perform this exercise. Up till then, everybody had been constructing the distribution functions independently for each parameter, even though the parameters are correlated. The accurate data also allowed us to perform a joint analysis, which provided one of the best constraints on the intrinsic brightness distribution and the FRB evolution with cosmic time. It showed that 30% of sources seemed to track cosmic star formation, while the remaining population was formed from a longer evolutionary channel. This is contrary to the prevailing understanding, but there is growing evidence that may support this.
